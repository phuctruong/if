## Abstract

Paper 7 proposed that dark-matter-like attraction and dark-energy-like expansion may arise
from two regimes of one nonequilibrium IF geometry. The present paper defines the first
observational tests of that hypothesis at galactic scales. It claims no positive IF result;
it is a preregistered observational-analysis protocol.

The central IF prediction is stronger than the statement that galaxy rotation curves can be
fitted without particle dark matter. Given a galaxy's baryonic mass distribution, distance,
inclination, environment, and cosmological epoch, one universal IF law must predict:

1. its resolved rotation curve;
2. the relation between baryonic and observed radial acceleration;
3. the normalization and scatter of the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation;
4. the effect of an external gravitational environment;
5. any redshift evolution of the characteristic acceleration scale;
6. gravitational lensing generated by the same metric potentials.

The primary local dataset is the SPARC rotation-curve compilation: 175 nearby late-type
galaxies with 3.6-micron photometry and high-quality H I and Hα rotation curves, spanning
broad ranges of stellar mass, surface brightness, morphology, and gas fraction.

The analysis will not optimize IF parameters independently for every galaxy. It will use a
hierarchical model with one global IF law, physically motivated nuisance parameters,
explicit observational uncertainties, held-out galaxies, and posterior predictive testing.
The principal comparator families are baryons-only Newtonian gravity, empirical
radial-acceleration relations, MOND-like laws, and conventional dark-matter halo models.

Environmental testing is mandatory. A published SPARC analysis reported a
greater-than-four-sigma external-field-effect signal using rotation curves together with
independent estimates of large-scale gravitational environment. A later hierarchical
reanalysis of the underlying radial-acceleration relation found only weak evidence for an
external-field effect — demonstrating that distance, inclination, stellar mass-to-light
ratio, functional form, and error modeling materially change the inference. The IF analysis
must reproduce both competing results before proposing a new environmental law.

The strongest prospective prediction concerns cosmic epoch. Paper 7's minimal Hubble-locked
prototype states:

\[
\boxed{\;a_{\mathrm{IF}}(z)=a_{\mathrm{IF},0}\,\frac{H(z)}{H_0}.\;}
\]

A recent intermediate-redshift analysis of 79 star-forming galaxies over \(0.33<z<1.44\)
reported that the radial-acceleration relation persists but has larger scatter and a
characteristic acceleration that increases with redshift. This is directionally relevant and
is **not** confirmation of IF Theory: it depends on three-dimensional forward modeling, gas
estimates, pressure-support corrections, selection, and an assumed mass decomposition — and
the IF formula was not preregistered before those data existed (`RETROFIT_FORECAST` applies
if it is claimed otherwise).

A second decisive test is gravitational lensing. Weak-lensing studies have extended
acceleration measurements far beyond optical rotation curves, but published analyses
disagree on the importance of galaxy type, isolation criteria, circumgalactic gas, and
stellar-mass consistency. One KiDS analysis found an early- versus late-type difference
difficult for simple universal modified-gravity laws; a later joint kinematic–lensing
analysis found that sufficiently isolated early- and late-type galaxies follow a common
relation when baryonic masses are modeled consistently.

The IF hypothesis survives only if the same field law predicts dynamics and lensing without
an independently fitted invisible halo or lensing correction. It is rejected if
galaxy-specific freedom approaches that of halo fitting, if environmental effects fail
independent prediction, if redshift evolution has the wrong sign or magnitude, or if the
metric potentials required by rotation curves do not reproduce gravitational lensing.

# 1. Introduction

Galaxies provide the most accessible observational setting in which the IF Unified Geometry
Hypothesis can be tested.

The discrepancy is conceptually simple. From the observed distribution of baryonic matter,
Newtonian gravity predicts a radial acceleration \(g_{\mathrm{bar}}(R)\). Observed orbital
motion implies:

\[
g_{\mathrm{obs}}(R)=\frac{V_{\mathrm{obs}}^2(R)}{R}.
\]

At high accelerations \(g_{\mathrm{obs}}\approx g_{\mathrm{bar}}\). At sufficiently low
accelerations \(g_{\mathrm{obs}}>g_{\mathrm{bar}}\).

The standard interpretation introduces a dark-matter halo whose gravitational field supplies
the difference:

\[
g_{\mathrm{obs}}=g_{\mathrm{bar}}+g_{\mathrm{halo}}.
\]

A modified-gravity interpretation instead changes the relationship between baryonic mass and
gravitational response:

\[
g_{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathcal G\left(g_{\mathrm{bar}},\ \text{environment},\ \text{epoch}\right).
\]

The IF proposal belongs to the second class at galactic scales, but it is more restrictive
than a static modified-gravity formula. Paper 7 proposed that the galactic law is one limit
of a dynamical field that also contributes to cosmic expansion. Therefore **the galaxy
acceleration scale, the environmental response, and the lensing law must all be locked to
the cosmological IF state.** The observational program below is designed to test that lock.

A good rotation-curve fit is necessary but insufficient. Flexible dark-matter halos,
empirical interpolation functions, altered stellar mass-to-light ratios, distance shifts,
inclination shifts, and gas corrections can all improve a fit. The correct question is:

> Does one low-dimensional IF law predict held-out galaxies and independent lensing or
> environmental observables better than its strongest alternatives?

The archived benchmark recorded in the Status note answers the *prior* generation of that
question with a flat no. Paper 8 is the specification of the next attempt, under fair rules
fixed in advance.

## 3.2 The radial acceleration relation

For each radial point:

\[
g_{\mathrm{obs}}(R)=\frac{V_{\mathrm{obs}}^2(R)}{R},\qquad
g_{\mathrm{bar}}(R)=\frac{V_{\mathrm{bar}}^2(R)}{R},
\]

with

\[
V_{\mathrm{bar}}^2=\Upsilon_d V_d^2+\Upsilon_b V_b^2+V_g^2,
\]

where \(V_d\) is the unit-mass stellar-disk contribution, \(V_b\) the bulge contribution,
\(V_g\) the gas contribution, and \(\Upsilon_d,\Upsilon_b\) the stellar mass-to-light ratios.

The original SPARC radial-acceleration analysis reported that 2,693 radial measurements in
153 galaxies follow a tight relationship between observed and baryonic acceleration across
diverse galaxies.

The existence of the relation does not distinguish uniquely between modified gravity,
baryon–halo coupling, galaxy-formation regularities, and sample or modeling effects. The IF
test concerns the relation's exact functional form, intrinsic scatter, residual
correlations, environmental dependence, redshift evolution, and lensing continuation.

## 3.3 Baryonic Tully–Fisher relation

In a deep low-acceleration IF regime, \(g_{\mathrm{obs}}^2\approx a_{\mathrm{IF}}\,
g_{\mathrm{bar}}\). For an isolated spherical asymptotic field,
\(g_{\mathrm{bar}}=GM_{\mathrm{bar}}/R^2\) and \(g_{\mathrm{obs}}=V_f^2/R\). Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\;V_f^4=GM_{\mathrm{bar}}\,a_{\mathrm{IF}}.\;}
\]

The local baryonic Tully–Fisher normalization estimates \(a_{\mathrm{IF},0}\). Under IF
unification, its redshift evolution is **not independently adjustable** — that is the whole
content of the unification claim, and a separate sector fit would fire `SECTOR_SPLIT_FIT`

# 5. Baryonic Mass Modeling

The most important astrophysical nuisance is the conversion from light to stellar mass.

For the stellar disk, \(V_{\star,d}^2(R)=\Upsilon_d V_d^2(R)\). For the bulge,
\(V_{\star,b}^2(R)=\Upsilon_b V_b^2(R)\). The gas term \(V_g^2(R)\) includes a declared
conversion from observed neutral gas to total cold-gas mass. Combined:

\[
\boxed{\;V_{\mathrm{bar}}^2=\Upsilon_d V_d^2+\Upsilon_b V_b^2+V_g^2.\;}
\]

The priors on \(\Upsilon_d\) and \(\Upsilon_b\) **must be identical** when comparing IF and
dark-halo models, unless a theory supplies an independent reason otherwise. The IF model is
not permitted to obtain an unfair advantage through broader stellar-mass priors. This
symmetry is the same rule under which the archived benchmark was run (one fitted
mass-to-light per galaxy for IF and MOND alike), and it is what makes the recorded numbers
binding on any successor law.

# 6. Distance and Inclination

Observed galaxy dynamics depend nontrivially on distance and inclination. Let catalog values
be \(D_0, i_0\), and model:

\[
D=D_0e^{\delta_D},\qquad i=i_0+\delta_i,
\]

with observational priors on \(\delta_D,\delta_i\) derived from catalog uncertainties. The
deprojected circular speed depends on inclination, \(V_{\mathrm{rot}}=V_{\mathrm{los}}/\sin i\),
and physical radius depends on distance, \(R=D\theta\).

Distance and inclination must be inferred **jointly** with the gravity model, not fixed at
best-estimate values and later treated as uncorrelated noise. Published hierarchical
analyses have shown that full treatment of these galaxy parameters materially alters both
the inferred radial-acceleration scatter and the strength of environmental evidence.

# 10. Prediction Versus Fit

The principal score is **not** the best in-sample likelihood. The analysis reports: held-out
predictive likelihood; posterior predictive residuals; calibration; parameter recovery;
effective parameter count; sensitivity to priors; and residual correlation with galaxy
properties.

A model with more parameters can obtain lower \(\chi^2\) without making a stronger physical
prediction. Symmetrically, the archived benchmark shows a model can lose on \(\chi^2\) *and*
lose on BIC — which removes the usual consolation that a simpler law was penalized unfairly.

# 11. Holdout Strategy

**11.1 Galaxy-level holdout.** Galaxies, not radial points, are divided into folds. This
prevents data from one galaxy appearing in both training and testing.

**11.2 Stratification.** Folds are balanced across stellar mass, surface brightness, gas
fraction, morphology, data quality, and environment.

**11.3 Extrapolation holdout.** Reserve extreme populations — lowest surface brightness,
highest gas fraction, smallest baryonic mass, strongest estimated environment — to measure
extrapolation rather than interpolation.

**11.4 Prequential prediction.** A stronger analysis orders galaxies under a frozen rule and
predicts each new galaxy from the posterior learned from previous systems. The sequence must
be fixed independently of model residuals.

# 12. Rotation-Curve Tests

**12.1 Pointwise residuals.** \(\Delta V_{jk}=V_{jk}^{\mathrm{obs}}-V_{jk}^{\mathrm{IF}}\),

environment, and redshift.

**12.2 Shape prediction.** A successful law must reproduce rotation-curve features associated

**12.3 Inner and outer regions.** Analyze baryon-dominated inner radii, transition radii, and

the inner shape.

**12.4 Residual independence.** Under a universal IF law, residuals should retain no strong
systematic dependence on stellar mass, disk scale length, surface brightness, gas fraction,
radius, or environment — except where the model predicts such dependence. Any residual trend
is evidence for missing physics.

# 15. Environmental Hysteresis

Then the effective environment is:

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf g_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}K_{\mathrm{IF}}(t-t')\,\mathbf g_{\mathrm{env}}(t')\,dt'.\;}

For exponential memory,

\[
K_{\mathrm{IF}}(\Delta t)=\frac{e^{-\Delta t/\tau_{\mathrm{IF}}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{IF}}},
\]

and the IF rotation law becomes
\(g_{\mathrm{obs}}=\mathcal G_{\mathrm{IF}}(g_{\mathrm{bar}},g_{\mathrm{eff}})\).

**15.1 Hysteresis prediction.** At equal present environmental field, galaxies with different

**15.2 Falsifier.** The dynamical-memory extension fails if present environment explains all
allowed effect; if inferred \(\tau_{\mathrm{IF}}\) is consistent with zero; if history
indicators add no held-out predictive value; or if the required relaxation differs
arbitrarily among galaxies.

# 18. Lensing Consistency

**18.1 Metric potentials.** Matter dynamics respond mainly to \(\Psi\); weak lensing responds
to \(\Phi+\Psi\). Define the gravitational slip

\[
\eta_{\mathrm{IF}}=\frac{\Phi}{\Psi}.
\]

The IF action must predict \(\eta_{\mathrm{IF}}\) from the same field parameters that govern
rotation curves.

**18.2 Excess surface density.** Galaxy–galaxy weak lensing measures

\[
\Delta\Sigma(R)=\bar\Sigma(<R)-\Sigma(R).
\]

The analysis must map the IF metric potentials to \(\Delta\Sigma_{\mathrm{IF}}(R)\) through
the relativistic lensing equation. An effective Newtonian density may be computed for
visualization,

\[
\nabla^2\left(\Phi+\Psi\right)=8\pi G\,\rho_{\mathrm{lens,eff}},
\]

but this density is **derived**, not a newly fitted halo.

**18.3 Dynamics–lensing lock.** The critical test: (1) fit IF parameters using rotation curves
only; (2) freeze them; (3) predict galaxy–galaxy lensing; (4) compare with observation. Then
reverse: fit lensing, predict rotation dynamics. The hypothesis fails if the two routes infer
incompatible field laws.

# 19. Existing Weak-Lensing Results

A KiDS-1000 analysis extended the radial-acceleration relation by approximately two decades
toward lower acceleration using weak lensing around isolated galaxies. It found broad
agreement with the modified-gravity predictions tested, but also reported a significant
difference between early- and late-type galaxies at fixed stellar mass, discussing
circumgalactic gas and galaxy-formation history as possible explanations.

A later joint kinematic and weak-lensing analysis used consistent stellar-population modeling
and a different conversion from excess surface density to radial acceleration. It found that
weak-lensing measurements smoothly extend the kinematic relation, and that sufficiently
isolated early- and late-type galaxies follow a common relation under its mass-modeling
assumptions.

The disagreement between these results establishes that the IF lensing test must explicitly
vary: isolation criteria; galaxy-type definition; stellar mass model; circumgalactic gas;
deprojection method; source-redshift calibration; photometric-redshift uncertainty;
satellite contamination; and lens miscentering.

# 20. Circumgalactic Baryons

A modified-gravity prediction depends on the complete baryonic distribution, not only stars
and cold disk gas:

\[
M_{\mathrm{bar}}=M_\star+M_{\mathrm{cold\ gas}}+M_{\mathrm{CGM}}+M_{\mathrm{other}}.
\]

At large radii, uncertain circumgalactic gas can affect both \(g_{\mathrm{bar}}\) and lensing
comparisons. The IF analysis will use explicit CGM scenarios — minimal gas, an
observationally motivated fiducial, and an extended high-gas model — and **propagate** the
uncertainty rather than absorbing it into the IF field.

A theory should not receive credit for explaining baryonic mass that was omitted from the
baryon model.

# 21. Strong-Lensing Extension

Strong lenses test projected gravity at smaller impact parameters and higher accelerations. A
viable IF law must recover the general-relativistic regime where \(g_{\mathrm{bar}}\gg
a_{\mathrm{IF}}\). For a lens system, predict the Einstein radius, image positions, time
delays, and stellar dynamics. The same \(\Upsilon_\star\) and the same IF field must explain
lensing and dynamics.

Strong-lensing analysis is secondary in Paper 8 because baryonic geometry, line-of-sight
structure, and mass-sheet degeneracies require dedicated treatment.
